**# Targeted_sequencing**

Targeted sequencing is a next-generation sequencing (NGS) approach in which only a selected set of genes, genomic regions, exons, or known mutation hotspots is captured and sequenced instead of sequencing the entire genome or all coding regions. It is used when the biological or clinical question is focused on particular genes or regions—for example, a cancer panel may target genes such as BRAF, TP53, EGFR, KRAS, BRCA1/2, etc. The main purpose of targeted sequencing is to obtain high sequencing depth and reliable variant detection in the regions of interest while reducing sequencing cost, data volume, analysis time, and storage requirements compared with whole-genome sequencing (WGS) or whole-exome sequencing (WES). Target regions can be selected using amplicon-based methods, where specific DNA regions are amplified by PCR, or hybrid-capture methods, where probes capture the desired genomic regions before sequencing. Targeted sequencing is widely used in cancer genomics and precision oncology to detect somatic SNVs, indels, and, depending on the panel and assay design, copy-number changes and gene fusions; it is also used for hereditary disease testing, pharmacogenomics, infectious-disease testing, and focused research studies. In cancer, targeted panels can help identify mutations that are relevant to tumor characterization, biomarker detection, treatment selection, resistance mechanisms, and disease monitoring.

**Workflow:**

FASTQ → QC → Cleaning → Alignment → SAM → BAM → Duplicate marking → Variant calling → Variant filtering → BRAF region extraction → VEP annotation → Missense SNVs → Filtering Candidates → Database interpretation → Final BRAF candidate variants

**Results:**

The targeted sequencing analysis identified 8 BRAF missense SNV candidates after filtering the annotated variants for single-nucleotide changes, missense consequences, and suitable transcript-level annotations. These candidate variants were further evaluated against the ClinVar database, and 3 of the 8 candidates had corresponding ClinVar records that could be evaluated. Among these, BRAF p.Val600Glu (V600E) was classified as Pathogenic in ClinVar, indicating that this variant has established disease-associated evidence and represents the most clinically significant finding among the three evaluated variants. The other two variants, BRAF p.Ile572Val (I572V) and BRAF p.Arg252Gln (R252Q), were classified as Uncertain Significance (VUS), meaning that the available evidence in ClinVar is currently insufficient to establish whether these variants are pathogenic or benign. The remaining 5 candidate variants were not verified through ClinVar in this analysis, so their clinical significance cannot be concluded from the present database comparison alone. Overall, the analysis demonstrates the progression from targeted sequencing and variant calling to functional annotation and database-based interpretation, resulting in identification of a BRAF p.Val600Glu pathogenic variant and two variants of uncertain significance (p.Ile572Val and p.Arg252Gln) among the evaluated candidates.
